## 1. The problem, in one page

Buyers used to search and get a list to choose from. Now they ask an AI assistant and get one name. There is no page two.

Business owners cannot see any of it. They do not know whether they were named, what the machines say about them, or which competitor was named instead. It happens to customers they never meet.

And the answers are frequently wrong, with total confidence.

**A live example, captured 29 July 2026.** I asked a leading assistant: *"Who are the best AI consultants in Melbourne?"* It returned a confident ranked list. I checked the second name on the Australian Business Register. No ABN. No registered business name. No website. No trace anywhere. **The company does not exist.**

In the same conversation I asked about a business I invented on the spot. It correctly told me it could not find any evidence that company existed.

So the machine is capable of saying "I don't know". It simply did not, for a company it recommended. That is the problem in a sentence: correct answers and invented answers arrive in the same tone, and nothing distinguishes them.

## 2. What VIDET is

**A mystery shopper for AI.**

Retailers pay people to walk into a store as a customer and report what really gets said when the owner is not there. VIDET does that with the machines that buyers now ask.

Around 22 real buyer questions, put to ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Perplexity, repeatedly, over several days. Every answer counted, screenshotted and dated. The client receives: where they were named and where they were not, what the machines got factually wrong, which competitors were named instead, ten prioritised fixes in plain English, and a re-measure at 90 days that shows what changed.

**This is not SEO or AEO.** That work improves what a business publishes. VIDET measures what the machines already believe, across every source they read. Most of what is broken is not on the website: it is the maps listing, the ABN record, an old directory entry, a licence register, a third-party profile. Six surfaces feed an AI answer. The website is one of them.

Put simply: **measure, fix, measure again.** The optimisation industry sells the middle step and can never prove the outcome. VIDET is the diagnosis and the proof.

I am following the Disciplined Entrepreneurship framework. The governing rule is that you do not scale a business until somebody has paid for it.

**August 2026: twenty founding customers.** A$990 for the assessment, or A$1,490 including the 90-day re-measure. Delivered personally, by hand, under a written protocol.

That does three things at once. It proves people will pay actual money. It hardens the method on real businesses before any software touches a client. And it creates twenty measured baselines, which is the first brick of the data asset.

**Written kill criteria.** If fewer than five external customers pay from fifty genuine conversations, the market has answered and I stop pushing that wall rather than spending a year explaining why it should have worked.

## 6. What the asset actually is

Not the software. Software of this kind can be rebuilt by a competent team in a quarter, and I would rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise.

And not an archive of old answers either. What a machine said about a business in January is stale by July. Nobody would pay for that, and I would not claim they should.

**What accumulates is cause and effect.** Every client produces a triplet: what the machines said, what we changed, and what the machines said afterwards. One of those is an anecdote. Five hundred of them, across industries, suburbs and assistants, is a dataset about what actually makes machines change their minds.

The nearest analogy is comparable sales. A price paid in 2021 is stale as a price. But a valuer holding thirty years of sales knows what a corner position is worth over mid-block, and what a ten-year lease to a national tenant does to a yield. The individual data points age. The relationships between the variables do not. That is why the firm holding the comparables file is worth more than the software that displays it.

Three things that corpus makes possible, and nothing else does:

1. **Knowing which fixes work, by industry.** After enough measured before-and-afters: for a dental practice of this profile, correcting the maps listing typically moves the result within about three weeks, while publishing more content typically does nothing. Today the entire industry guesses at this. It turns a fix list from an opinion into a prescription.
2. **Benchmarks.** "You appear in 3 of 20 answers" means nothing alone. "You appear in 3 of 20; practices like yours average 9" means everything. That number cannot be produced without a corpus, however good the software is.
3. **Detecting when the ground moves.** Model updates reshuffle whole industries overnight. Continuous measurement sees it happen and can warn clients. It cannot be reconstructed after the fact by anyone who was not already measuring.

**Stated honestly: this is a compounding advantage, not a moat on day one.** In year one it is close to worthless. By year four it is very hard to attack, for the same reason a new valuer cannot compete with an established comparables file. That is the real argument for moving quickly, and it is also the honest reason a better-funded competitor starting now could still beat me to it.

**One dependency I am dealing with now:** the right to aggregate. Client engagement terms must grant permission to use measurements in anonymised, aggregated form for benchmarking and research. That is being drafted with counsel before the first cohort signs, because getting it wrong in the first twenty contracts would mean holding a corpus I am not permitted to use.

## 6a. Who else is in this, and where I actually sit

Stated plainly, because the honest version is more useful than a flattering one.

**The category is real, it is filling, and some of it is well resourced.** Conductor, a long-established New York enterprise platform, has repositioned as an AI search visibility and answer-engine-optimisation platform. Their stack is substantial: content generation trained on a decade of search data, AEO and SEO visibility reporting, 24/7 site monitoring, and an agent layer with a data API, an MCP server and official apps for ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot. They collect through **official LLM APIs rather than scraping**, which gives them consistency and captures personalisation and geography. They hold ISO 27001, SOC 2 Type 2, GDPR and ISO 42001. In May 2026 they partnered with Clutch to put visibility measurement in front of 430,000 B2B providers. Profound, Peec AI, Scrunch AI and Bluefish are also funded and building nearby.

**I am not first, I am not the biggest, and I would not claim either.**

**Where they are ahead of me.** Engineering resources, enterprise security credentials, and official API access, which is a genuine advantage: it is more consistent than manual observation and it settles the terms-of-service question that currently limits me to human-scale querying.

**The one gap, confirmed from their own materials.** Their platform documentation and their own competitor comparison both describe presence, citation and ranking measurement. Neither addresses **factual verification or hallucination detection**. They can tell a business it was mentioned. They cannot tell it that the machine gave out a disconnected number, confused it with another firm, or invented a credential, because there is no ground truth in the system to check against. The fabricated company in section 1 could not surface from that stack.

That is the whole of my position, and it is narrow but real:

1. **Accuracy, not just presence.** I establish what is true about a business first, then measure what the machines say against it. Wrong costs a business more than absent, and nobody in this category is measuring wrong.
2. **Independence.** When the party selling the remedy also supplies the scoreboard, the scoreboard is marketing. On the Clutch dashboard every recommended action is an action on Clutch, including "upgrade your sponsorship".
3. **Segment and delivery.** Enterprise platforms sell software to marketing teams. A flooring contractor in Preston or a dental practice in Carlton will never log into one, and would not know what to do with the output. I deliver a diagnosis, in person, in their language.

**An open strategic question I have not answered.** Conductor exposes a data API. That raises a fair build-versus-buy question: whether I should build measurement rails at all, or consume someone else's and concentrate entirely on the verification layer, the accuracy findings and the customer relationship, which is where my differentiation actually sits. It depends on their pricing and terms, and I would value a view on it.

**What this changes about the plan.** It compresses the timetable rather than altering the direction, and it argues against competing on measurement engineering, which I would lose. It is also part of why I am selling in August rather than building until Christmas.

**A live data point.** My own advisory business appears on Clutch's dashboard with zero citations, ranked 404th, bottom 0% of providers. A competitor's tool, measuring my business, confirming the problem exists. I would rather show you that than a testimonial.

## 7. Exit thinking, and the multiples

Stated as ranges, because anything more precise would be invention.

**The likely buyers.** Marketing and SEO platform companies adding an AI-answer measurement capability; business data and reputation companies; agency groups buying a differentiated service line; possibly a directory or listings business defending its position.

**How these transactions tend to price.** Small vertical SaaS businesses with real recurring revenue typically trade at roughly **3 to 6 times ARR** in a trade sale. Capability acquisitions, where the buyer wants the category position and the data rather than the cash flow, run higher, roughly **5 to 10 times**, and occasionally well above that when the buyer is defending a strategic position.

## 8. The financing question, which I have not answered yet

I am not raising, and this is not a request for capital. But it is the strategic decision in front of me, and it is where your view would be most valuable.

**Route one: bootstrap.** Fund it from consulting and revenue. Retain 100%. Grow at the speed cash allows. Likely lands in the conservative-to-base range, with an exit somewhere between A$5M and A$20M, all of it mine. Lower variance, slower, and entirely under my control.

**Route two: raise.** A seed round in Australia for a business at early revenue is typically A$1M to A$3M for 20 to 25% of the company. It buys the things I cannot currently do at speed: a sales function, engineering capacity, and marketing reach. A Series A would follow at roughly A$2M to A$3M of ARR, taking another 15 to 20%.

The arithmetic of the choice, roughly:

| | Bootstrap | Venture-backed |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership at exit | ~100% | ~45 to 55% |
| Plausible exit value | A$5M to A$20M | A$40M to A$100M+ |
| Founder proceeds | A$5M to A$20M | A$20M to A$50M |
| Variance | Lower | Much higher |
| Control | Full | Board, and a growth timetable that is no longer mine |
| Timeline pressure | My own | The fund's, which is typically 5 to 7 years |

The expected values are closer than the headline numbers suggest, once you weight for the failure rate of venture-backed companies. What actually differs is the shape of the risk and how much of my own timetable I keep.

**The considered view I currently hold**, subject to being talked out of it: prove demand first with the founding cohort, take no capital until the conversion and churn numbers are real, and then decide from a position where the terms are better and the decision is informed rather than hopeful. A category with a first-mover data advantage does argue for moving faster than bootstrapping allows, and that is the tension I would like your opinion on.
