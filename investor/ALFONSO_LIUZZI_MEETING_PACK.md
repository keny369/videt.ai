# Meeting pack — Alfonso Liuzzi, 30 minutes

Prepared 29 July 2026. For a friendly feedback and brainstorming session, not a raise. Walk it in order. Everything here is either public, checkable, or your own honest position.

---

## 1. Who you are talking to (read this first)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Director and owner, Liuzzi Property Group, 20+ years. Family group founded 1950s by Fred Liuzzi. |
| **What the group does** | Retail and commercial property across the Australian Eastern Seaboard, plus operating businesses: hospitality (Olympic Hotel, Braybrook Hotel), childcare, construction, electrical, automotive repairs, beverages, manufacturing. |
| **His background** | **Law and I.T.** He specialises in retail and commercial property: leasing, acquisitions, developments. |
| **Governance** | Sits on the CATA board, so he understands what a board seat actually costs a person in time. |
| **Based** | Level 2, 62 Albert Street, Preston. |

**Three consequences for how you pitch.**

1. **He is not a non-technical property guy.** Law plus I.T. means he will follow the technical argument and he will ask sharp questions about IP, defensibility and legal exposure. Do not dumb it down and do not bluff. If you do not know, say so.
2. **He thinks like an asset owner, not a startup investor.** Property people ask: what is the asset, what is the yield, what happens in a downturn, how long does it last, who else can take it. Answer in those terms and you will sound like a peer. Talk about hockey sticks and you will sound like everyone else who has pitched him.
3. **The group owns businesses in at least four of your target verticals.** Childcare, hospitality, automotive, electrical. He does not need the problem explained in the abstract. He can be shown it, live, on something he owns.

---

## 2. How to play the meeting

**Consult him, do not pitch him.** He offered feedback and brainstorming. The fastest way to lose a man of his standing is to be sold to by a friend. The way to win him is to show him something genuinely interesting, be unusually honest about what could kill it, and ask him two or three questions only he can answer. If he wants a role, he will raise it himself.

**Spend your first two minutes showing, not telling.** You have a live finding. Use it.

**Ask for his judgement, not his money.** You are not raising. Say so early; it relaxes the room and makes everything after it more credible.

**The single best move available to you:** offer to run a free assessment on one of the group's operating businesses. Say the childcare centre or one of the pubs. It costs you a few hours, it gives him something concrete, and it converts an abstract conversation into evidence about a business he owns. It is also the most natural possible route to him becoming an advocate.

## 3. The 15-minute walkthrough

### Minute 0 to 2. Show the problem

Open a laptop. Type live: *"Who are the best AI consultants in Melbourne?"* Let it answer. Then:

> "It gave me a ranked list. Confident, specific. I checked the second one on the government business register. No ABN, no business name, no website. It does not exist. In the same conversation I asked about a company I invented on the spot, and it correctly told me it could not find it. So it can say 'I do not know'. It just did not, for the one it recommended to me."

Then the turn:

> "Now think about the pubs, or the childcare centre. Somebody asks an assistant for a recommendation, gets one name, and rings it. Nobody at the business ever knows the conversation happened."

### Minute 2 to 4. What VIDET is, in one breath

> "VIDET is a mystery shopper for AI. We ask the machines what a real buyer would ask, dozens of times over several days, count every answer, screenshot it, and show the business what the machines actually say about them. Then what to fix, and then we measure again to prove it changed."
>
> "It is not SEO or AEO. That work improves what you publish. We measure what the machines already believe, and most of what is wrong is not on the website. It is the maps listing, the ABN record, an old directory entry, a licence register."

### Minute 4 to 6. Why now, and why me

> "Two things had to be true at once. Buyers had to start asking machines instead of searching, which is happening now. And somebody had to care enough about evidence to check the machine's homework, which almost nobody does."
>
> "I have thirty years of software behind me, Scrivener for Windows through to exit, two companies founded and sold, and I have spent the last few years building for exactly these kinds of businesses. I am not learning this from a YouTube video."

### Minute 6 to 10. How I am proving it

This is the part he will respect most, because it is the part most founders skip.

> "I am following a method called Disciplined Entrepreneurship. The rule is simple: do not build the business until somebody pays for it. So I am not raising, I am not building features, I am selling twenty assessments this August at A$990, or A$1,490 with a re-measure at ninety days. Delivered by hand, by me."
>
> "That does three things. It proves people will pay. It shakes the method down on real businesses before software touches a client. And it gives me twenty measured baselines, which is the beginning of the data asset."
>
> "The kill criteria are written down. If I cannot get five external paid customers out of fifty genuine conversations, the market has answered and I stop pushing that wall."

### Minute 10 to 13. The numbers, simply

Do not hand him a spreadsheet. Say this:

> "Three horizons.
>
> **Now.** Twenty founding assessments in August. Floor five paid, target ten to fifteen, stretch twenty. That is roughly A$5,000 at the floor and A$25,000 to A$30,000 at the stretch. Small money. It is not meant to be big money, it is meant to be proof.
>
> **Next.** The platform is about seventy-five percent built. Once it runs, the same loop becomes a subscription instead of a one-off, because the measurement has to repeat to be worth anything. A few hundred businesses on a modest monthly fee is a real business. A few thousand is a substantial one.
>
> **After that.** The asset is not the software. Anyone can write software. The asset is the accumulated record: what the machines said about thousands of businesses over years, what was changed, and what happened next. Nobody can go back and collect that retrospectively. Whoever starts first owns the longest history, and that is the part that gets bought."

### Minute 13 to 15. Status and the ask

> "Where it stands. Company incorporated, Videt AI Pty Ltd. Trade marks with an IP lawyer, Australia first then US, UK and Europe inside the priority window. Platform about seventy-five percent. Method written down and already run on real businesses. First customers this August."
>
> "What I would value from you is not money. It is judgement. You have run operating businesses across a lot of industries for a long time. I would like to know where you think this breaks."

## 4. Questions he will almost certainly ask, and your answers

**"What actually is the asset here? What am I looking at?"**
> "Three layers. The method, which is written down and repeatable. The brand and trade marks, which is why I have filed early. And the one that matters: the measurement record. Every client gives us a dated baseline and a re-measure. That record cannot be built retrospectively and it compounds. Software gets copied in a quarter. Six years of measured before-and-after does not."

**"Why can't OpenAI or Google just do this themselves?"**
> "They could, and they might, for their own platform. But they have no incentive to publish how often their own answers are wrong, and a business does not want each platform marking its own homework. They want one independent read across all of them. That is the same reason auditors exist inside industries that could technically audit themselves."

**"What stops a competitor copying you?"**
> "In the short term, nothing structural, and I would not pretend otherwise. What I have is a head start, the discipline that most of the market does not have the patience for, and the accumulating data. Most people in this space sell optimisation because it is easier to sell and impossible to disprove. Measurement is harder, and it exposes you when it does not work. That filters out most competitors on temperament rather than capability."

**"How do you know anyone will pay?"**
> "I do not yet. That is precisely what August is for. Fifty genuine conversations, twenty places, and written kill criteria if it does not land. I would rather find out in thirty days for the price of my own time than in two years for the price of somebody's investment."

**"What kills this?"**
> Give him three, honestly. It buys more credibility than any answer on this page.
> "One: the machines get materially more accurate and the problem shrinks. It will shrink, but slowly, and it shrinks last in exactly the small-business corner where my clients live.
> Two: a platform ships a free console that does eighty percent of this. Plausible in a few years. It also validates the category and makes me an acquisition target rather than a casualty.
> Three: the one that actually worries me, which is that owners simply do not care enough to pay. That is not a technology risk, it is a demand risk, and it is the only one August answers."

**"What are you doing for money in the meantime?"**
> "Advisory work through Lumen & Lever. That is honest: consulting cash funds this. Which is part of why I would still be interested in the AI and automation work your team raised for the leases, if that is live."

**"How much time is this taking? Are you doing it alone?"**
> "Alone by design at the moment. The delivery is about six to eight hours per client, and I will not sell more than I can deliver personally, which is why the cohort is capped at twenty. When the platform carries the measurement, that number changes."

**"What do you want from me?"**
> "Nothing today except your read on it. If it turns out to be useful for you to be more involved later, I would rather that came from you seeing it work than from me asking now."

**"Have you protected the name and the IP?"**
> He has a law background, so be precise.
> "Videt AI Pty Ltd is incorporated. The word mark VIDET is with an IP lawyer, Australia first because the Australian filing date starts a six-month priority window for the US, UK and Europe. We are also looking at the tagline as a second mark. The register came back clear on the word. What I cannot protect is the idea, which is why the data record matters more than the method."

**"What are the margins?"**
> "The assessment is almost entirely my time, so today it is a services margin, not a software margin. That is fine for the proof phase and it is not the business. The subscription is where the economics get interesting, because the same measurement engine serves every client."

**"Who is the customer, precisely?"**
> "Owner-led businesses where one job or client is worth far more than the fee. Specialist trades, professional practices, clinics. Frankly, the childcare centre and the pubs are exactly the profile."

## 5. Questions he might ask that would catch you out

- **"What is your unfair advantage?"** Do not say thirty years of experience. Say: "Being early, being disciplined about evidence when the market is not, and the record that builds. And honestly, being able to build it myself means I can move without a budget or a team."
- **"Who else has told you this is a good idea, and who has told you it is not?"** Have a real answer for both sides. Naming a sceptic makes you credible.
- **"What is the number that tells you this is working?"** "Paid external customers. Not conversations, not interest. Five is survival, ten to fifteen is a business, twenty means I raise the price."
- **"Why hasn't someone already done this?"** "Parts of it exist. The optimisation vendors are well funded. What nobody is doing is the measurement and the verification, because it is slower, it is harder to sell, and it can prove you wrong."
- **"What would you do with a million dollars?"** Be careful. "Not much, today. Money does not fix the open question, which is demand. After August it buys delivery capacity and the platform build, and I would want to know what it is buying before I took it."
- **"Are you the right person to run this at scale?"** "I have taken software to a million-plus users and out the other side. What I have not done is build a sales organisation, and that is where I would want help before I would want capital."

## 6. If he raises a board seat or equity

Do not offer equity in this meeting. Offering it unprompted reads as buying his time, and it prices something you cannot value yet. If he raises it:

> "I would take your counsel gladly. I would rather we did ninety days informally first, so you can see whether the thing is real and whether I am any good to work with, and then we make it proper if it still makes sense to you."

That is the answer a serious operator wants to hear. It also protects you: a cap table decision made in the first fifteen minutes of a friendly chat is a decision made badly.

---

## 7. The Lumen & Lever question

If he raises the AI and automation work for the leases, say yes and be straightforward about why:

> "Yes, I would be interested. I will be honest with you, consulting cash is what funds VIDET while it proves itself, so work like that is useful to me. And the lease automation is a genuinely good fit for what I do. The only thing I would flag is that from August my delivery weeks are committed to the founding cohort, so we would want to scope it around that."

Do not pretend you do not need the money. He has run businesses for thirty years and will read it instantly. Candour is the stronger position.

---

## 8. What not to say

- **No valuation talk, no exit numbers.** If he asks where it goes: "the honest answer is a trade sale in a few years, but I would be making it up if I put a number on it today."
- **No promised platform dates.** Seventy-five percent built is a fact. "Live in three weeks" is a hostage.
- **No naming the real company from the demo.** Name only the one that does not exist.
- **No architecture, no methodology internals.** He is a friend, not a signed party, and it is not what he is offering to help with anyway.
- **Do not oversell the twenty places to him.** He is not a customer. Scarcity language aimed at a friend reads as technique.

---

## 9. The one thing to make sure you ask him

> "You have run operating businesses across a lot of industries. If you were a customer, what would make you pay for this, and what would make you ignore it entirely?"

Then be quiet and take notes. That question is why he agreed to the meeting, it is the thing he is uniquely qualified to answer, and his answer is worth more than anything you will say in the other twenty-nine minutes.

**And then, before you leave:**

> "Would you let me run one on the childcare centre, or one of the pubs? No charge, no obligation. You will see exactly what a client gets, and you will find out what the machines are telling people about a business you own."
